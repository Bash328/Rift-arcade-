# Fix empty rooms, then tune Rift

The biggest lever for Rift Arcade is not netcode or rendering. It is the empty-room problem: with fewer than two players Cube Tag does not advance, so a lone visitor sees a dead game. Labeled server-side bots that backfill public quick-play rooms fix that directly, and they are also the prerequisite for portals, leaderboards and new modes to matter. The notes found no measured retention uplift from bots in any small indie game, so this ranking is judgment built on shipped-game precedent ([Checkpoint Gaming](https://checkpointgaming.net/news/2021/05/destruction-allstars-to-add-bots-to-help-with-matchmaking/)). The network stack already matches Colyseus's recommended shape (fixed timestep, patch rate equal to tick rate, reconnection hold), so the remaining wins are cheap hardening: a jitter-adaptive interpolation delay, a stale-input policy, and a visibility handler. On rendering, the code is already well tiered, and the notes recommend measuring draw calls on a real phone first, then fixing bloom resolution, scenery instancing and the particle pool. For new content, a mode field with per-round rotation plus Orb Rush and Infection gives the most variety per day of work. The evidence is thin throughout. Most sources are blogs, forum posts and vendor pages, no mobile GPU timings exist, and all fun ratings are design judgment, not playtest data.

## Empty rooms and name safety come before everything else

The retention notes read the repo and found that Cube Tag's `step` returns early with under two players, `maxClients` is 8, and names are only `trim().slice(0,12)` with no filtering. Bot backfill is the standard remedy for low populations. Destruction AllStars added bots to fill queues that real players did not ([Checkpoint Gaming](https://checkpointgaming.net/news/2021/05/destruction-allstars-to-add-bots-to-help-with-matchmaking/)), and a two-person indie team's launch stalled below one full server with no bots ([Steam thread](https://steamcommunity.com/app/3151710/discussions/0/4635988976587613313)). That second source is anecdotal. The proposed design needs no network client. A bot is a `Player` entry with a synthetic id and a `bot` flag, fed a generated `MoveInput` before the `stepEntity` loop. A bot that is "it" chases the nearest non-it player, and one that is not "it" flees toward the arena center. Both get a 150-250 ms reaction delay so they stay beatable. The rule is to backfill to about four occupants after a 5-8 second wait, drop one bot per human join so `maxClients` never blocks people, skip private rooms unless the host opts in, and label bots visibly. Disclosure is cheap, and the Fall Guys experience shows hidden matchmaking choices carry reputational risk ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/mediatonic-s-plan-to-exile-i-fall-guys-i-cheaters-to-a-cheaters-only-queue-backfired)). The notes found no sources on bot-disclosure norms or on Colyseus server-side bot patterns, so the bot design is inference.

Name filtering is the safety baseline and a precondition for any leaderboard. The notes propose NFKC normalization, stripping control, zero-width and right-to-left characters, a leetspeak-aware blocklist with a "Player" fallback, and a "hide names" toggle like Altitude's ([Altitude wiki](https://altitudegame.com/wiki/index.php/Parental_Controls)). Names are the only user-generated content, which also keeps Rift clear of chat approval rules: CrazyGames' only chat rule is supporting a DisableChat preference ([CrazyGames](https://docs.crazygames.com/requirements/intro/)), while Poki requires prior approval for chat or user content ([Poki deals](https://sdk.poki.com/deals)). Preset emotes are a low-effort social layer: an integer id from a fixed list of 6-8, rate-limited to one per 1-2 seconds, with no moderation burden.

Persistence is the next step. The recommended design is a random `pid` in localStorage plus a server-side SQLite file holding wins, streak and unlocked cosmetics, with only the server awarding wins. SQLite is free, fits a single Node process and needs no accounts, but it loses data on ephemeral-disk hosts, and the real deploy host could not be determined from the repo. Node's built-in `node:sqlite` is only a "release candidate" in the Node 25 docs, and one third-party source disagrees about its status on the Node 22 floor in `package.json` ([Node docs](https://nodejs.org/download/release/latest-v25.x/docs/api/sqlite.html)). Daily and weekly boards should count only rounds with at least two humans, so bots and solo play cannot farm them. Streaks and cosmetic unlocks on the existing `PETS` and hue system are standard mechanics, but the notes found no evidence quantifying their uplift in small browser games. Mobile retention benchmarks exist, roughly 30-40% day-1 for casual puzzle titles ([Juego Studio](https://www.juegostudio.com/blog/how-to-increase-user-retention-and-increase-your-games-lifetime)), but they are not web-specific and should not be treated as targets. Storing a `pid` server-side counts as a persistent identifier under the amended COPPA rule, whose compliance date passed on 22 April 2026 ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/ftc-publishes-final-coppa-rule-amendments)). The notes rely on law-firm summaries, not FTC text, and recommend legal advice if children are the audience.

Spectating is the one retention item the notes advise skipping. Cube Tag has no elimination and allows join-in-progress, and an auto-looping round already works as a rematch, so the room code is the rematch handle. A post-round summary built from the existing `winner` and `score` fields, with a prominent share button, covers most of the value. Voidfall is different, because eliminated players wait. There a spectate camera or a "ghost" role matters more, and the new-modes section covers it.

## Netcode is sound, so spend effort on jitter, backlogs and measurement

The server already runs `setFixedTimestep` with a 33 ms patch rate, a 120 msg/s cap against roughly 30 inputs/s, and `allowReconnection(client, 30)`. Colyseus's default patch rate is 50 ms ([Colyseus Room docs](https://docs.colyseus.io/room)), so Rift deliberately pays about 50% more bandwidth for smoothness. The notes' own arithmetic, which is an estimate and not a measurement, puts that at about 10-15 KB/s downstream per client at 8-12 players, and roughly 1.4 Mbit/s of egress for a 12-player room. Throughput is therefore not the risk. Latency, loss and stalls are. The notes also flag that `maxClients` is 8 against a stated 8-12 target, and that Voidfall already allows 12.

The highest-value client change is a jitter-adaptive interpolation delay. Remote players use a static 100 ms delay (130 ms on phones), which matches Gaffer's rule of about three times the snapshot interval, roughly 150 ms at 30 snapshots per second, but leaves nothing for jitter ([Gaffer On Games](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_interpolation/)). Valve's default is also 100 ms ([Valve wiki](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Source_Multiplayer_Networking)). The suggestion is to track an EWMA of snapshot inter-arrival gaps and set the delay between 66 and 200 ms, changing it slowly. The clamp numbers are the note author's suggestion, not sourced, and the notes could not verify whether the Colyseus predict SDK lets the delay change at runtime. Reading `node_modules/@colyseus/sdk/predict` is the first step.

Two server-side issues come from reading the code. A stale-input backlog drains at two inputs per tick, so a 30-input backlog takes about a second to clear while the client has already moved on, producing a visible snap. Discarding the oldest inputs once more than 6-8 are pending would fix that. The same drain rate is also an exploit. A client sending 60 inputs/s, under the 120 cap, keeps the queue at the 2-per-tick drain and moves at up to double speed continuously. A token bucket that refills one step per tick with a debt of about three closes it. Both are derived from code reading and have not been reproduced. The notes also found no `visibilitychange` handling. If the predictor returns many catch-up steps after a tab resume, the client could emit a burst that trips the 120 msg/s disconnect. Whether it does is unverified, because the SDK behavior is unread. Resetting the predictor clock on resume is a few lines. Mobile browsers also kill background sockets more aggressively than desktop, but the notes found only desktop sources, so the existing reconnect is the main safety net. The client shows only "Disconnected," and a "Reconnecting..." state is a UX gap. A disconnected player's cube also stays in play for up to 30 seconds and can stall a round while remaining "it."

WebTransport is not worth adopting yet. It would cure TCP head-of-line blocking on lossy mobile links, but Colyseus labels its `h3-transport` "Experimental - hasn't been battle tested" ([Colyseus WebTransport docs](https://docs.colyseus.io/server/transport/webtransport)). Browser support is reported as newly baseline in Safari 26.4, but only by secondary sources ([VideoSDK](https://www.videosdk.live/developer-hub/webtransport/safari-webtransport)). The sensible path is to measure WebSocket stalls first. Measurement is itself a gap. The existing load test checks update regularity for two clients with no latency or loss shaping, no pass/fail exit code, and no 12-bot run. The notes rank the upgrades as threshold assertions, a delay-injecting TCP proxy, a 12-bot random-walk run reporting tick time and bytes per client, and AFK and reconnect modes. The HUD thresholds they propose (a weak-connection badge above 250 ms RTT or 40 ms jitter) are explicitly heuristics, since no citable "feels smooth" numbers exist for tag games. Hosting region is unresearched, and a 100 ms round-trip floor will outweigh any tuning of 33 ms ticks.

## Rendering: measure on a real phone, then fix bloom, scenery and particles

The rendering notes agree the code is already well tiered: DPR caps of 1.25 on the low-end tier and 1.5 on desktop, half the MSAA samples on phones, one directional shadow, a pooled particle system, and a frame-time governor with hysteresis (60 slow frames to drop, 600 fast frames to rise). The recommended first step is not an optimization. It is extending the existing debug HUD to print `renderer.info.render.calls`, triangles and texture counts, then reading them per arena on a real phone, because no real draw-call counts or mobile GPU timings were measured. The sources are blogs and forum anecdotes. One forum user saw `UnrealBloomPass` cut an i7 laptop to about 40 fps ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/unreal-bloom-optimize/35476)). Another saw Intel Mac Safari go from about 20 fps at full width to 60 fps at about 400 px, so cost tracks canvas size ([three.js forum](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/unrealbloompass-poor-performance-on-macos-safari-with-intel/42606)). A tips blog claims half-resolution post-processing "can roughly double frame rate in fill-rate-bound scenes" ([Utsubo](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips)). That blog is not official documentation, and the Discover three.js author says his tips are not all experimentally verified ([Discover three.js](https://discoverthreejs.com/tips-and-tricks/)).

With that caveat, the fix list is concrete. On the phone tier, drop MSAA and consider an 8-bit target instead of half-float, which is one line in the hand-built render target, but banding in sky and fog must be checked because the sun and hemisphere lights exceed 1.0. Run bloom at half the canvas size. Add bloom-off and shadow-downgrade levels to `adaptQuality`, which currently stops at a 0.5 scale floor with no further lever. Cheaper shadow filtering such as `PCFShadowMap` and blob shadows on the lowest tier are plausible wins with no measured delta. The draw-call work is more certain. Scenery builds a separate mesh, geometry and material for each rock and tree, and `neonBox` adds a mesh plus an edge `LineSegments` per box. Each of those is drawn twice when shadows are on. Converting them to `InstancedMesh` or merged geometry is the notes' highest-value draw-call fix. The particle pool gives each of 300 sprites its own material, so every live particle is a separate draw call. Moving to one `Points` or an instanced quad, or capping particles on phones, avoids pushing past the roughly 100-call mobile budget the notes cite ([Utsubo](https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips)).

The smaller items are context-loss handling, which matters because mobile GPUs can drop the context when backgrounded, and one-time hitches when a canvas-painted floor or the ice environment map builds on arena swap. Those could be pre-painted in the lobby. Device tiering uses `(pointer: coarse)`, which misreads touch laptops and weak non-touch laptops, so `hardwareConcurrency` or a short startup benchmark would refine it. An optional 30 fps battery-saver mode is suggested, with no measured data and no web thermal API found. WebGPU should wait. The post stack is WebGL addon code that a WebGPU move would rewrite, a WebGL2 fallback would still be needed, and no benchmark shows a gain for a scene this light. The browser support matrix the notes found is itself unverified and conflicting across sources.

## New modes: rotate rule sets inside one room

The game-ideas notes argue the evidence supports short rounds, instant joins and variety, with thin support for the finer design choices. Io games need to be graspable in about 60 seconds, with short readable party codes and guest play ([Flurry talk](https://gitnation.com/contents/lessons-from-7-years-of-io-games-what-works-what-doesnt-work-where-to-go-from-here)). Fall Guys cut its 100-player rounds because they stopped being readable at that size ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/fall-guys-was-almost-called-stumble-chums-1844794964)), seen only as a search snippet. Stumble Guys is described as repetitive over time ([Southerner Online](https://southerneronline.com/3811/arts-entertainment/the-rise-and-fall-of-stumble-guys/)), which is the argument for variety. Rift already matches much of this, with 4-letter codes, guest names and 90-second rounds, and it already varies the arena per round. The natural extension is a `mode` field with per-round rotation inside the same room, which keeps the code stable. Cube Tag currently admits mid-round joiners at score 0, which would be unfair in scored modes, so joiners should spectate until the next round as Voidfall does.

The ranking in the notes is by value per day of work. Orb Rush comes first (about one day): orbs spawn at free points, touching one scores, and a golden orb is worth three. It needs one server-owned entity, never eliminates anyone, and uses the stick alone. Infection comes next (about one day): it generalizes `state.it` to a per-player `infected` flag, and the eliminated rejoin as hunters, which removes the dead-time problem. King of the Hill follows (about one day), with a moving, telegraphed zone. It is weak at two players, and the Halopedia post-mortem points warn that fixed-schedule hills penalize late arrivals ([Halopedia](https://www.halopedia.org/King_of_the_Hill)). Falling Hazards is a low-risk fallback for 8 or more players. Sumo Push has the highest fun potential but the highest technical risk. Neither step function handles player-versus-player contact, and putting it in the shared step risks rubber-banding in prediction. A server-only velocity kick is the cheaper path. Hot Potato and Checkpoint Race rank lowest. Hot Potato is an elimination mode, and a race has little player interaction. Everything except dodgeball fits one stick plus an optional jump, which matters because the protocol carries only `moveX`, `moveY` and `jump`. The stick-only point is supported by general one-handed-design sources ([Geek Extreme](https://www.geekextreme.com/simple-game-mechanics-one-button-signals/)). All effort estimates and fun ratings are the note author's, and the cited Smash Karts and Tagpro facts come from roundups and reviews, not developer postmortems.

## Conclusion

The notes change the shape of the problem. Rift's technical foundations are in better shape than the question assumes, with prediction, shared deterministic stepping, tiered rendering and reconnection already in place. The real exposure is a product failure mode, a game that looks broken to the first visitor, plus two small hardening gaps that the netcode notes derived from code and did not confirm by running the game: an input-rate speed exploit and an unverified tab-resume burst. Fixing those costs days, not weeks.

The honest limit is evidence. Nothing in the notes measures Rift on a real phone, nothing quantifies what bots, streaks, cosmetics or emotes do for retention in a small browser game, and the portal economics, analytics pricing and several browser-support claims come from conflicting or secondary sources. The sensible sequence is therefore to instrument first (HUD, load test, a server-side return-rate counter), ship bots and name safety, and let the measured numbers decide how far to go on rendering, persistence and portals. Verification gaps that block specific work include the unread `@colyseus/sdk/predict` source, the unread `MyRoomState.ts` schema types behind the bandwidth estimate, and the unread `createDebugHud` in `kit.js`.
